## 2.26

**Solved · high confidence.** Combine the ideal-gas law with the calorically perfect internal-energy expression for air in an open room.

**Assumptions**

- The source scan actually prints “interval energy”; this is interpreted as internal energy, consistent with U in the chapter formulas. The printed problem text is retained.
- The room volume is fixed and the room communicates with the atmosphere, maintaining its pressure at the stated outside pressure while its gas amount changes with temperature.
- Use an ideal gas with temperature-independent air heat-capacity ratio gamma=1.40 from Appendix 15. Internal energy means the conventional thermal ideal-gas energy U=nu Cv T, with its chosen zero.
- Appendix 28 supplies normal atmospheric pressure 101300 Pa.

For a given temperature the open room contains the number of moles required by its fixed pressure and volume.

$$
\nu(T)=\frac{pV}{RT},\qquad C_V=\frac{R}{\gamma-1}.
$$

Insert this amount into the internal energy. The explicit temperature cancels because hotter air partly leaves the room.

$$
U=\nu C_VT=\frac{pV}{\gamma-1},\qquad \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_{p,V,\gamma}=0.
$$

Evaluate with the specified room volume and the Appendix pressure and heat-capacity ratio.

$$
U=\frac{101300\times40}{1.40-1}\ {\rm J}=1.013\times10^7\ {\rm J}=10.13\ {\rm MJ}.
$$

**Results**

Thermal internal energy of the room air

$$
U=\frac{pV}{\gamma-1}=10.13\ {\rm MJ}
$$

**Limitations**

- If heat capacity changes appreciably with temperature, Cv T is replaced by an integral and the cancellation is no longer exact.
- The result uses the conventional ideal-gas thermal-energy zero; it does not include molecular binding or rest energy.

## 2.27

**Solved · high confidence.** Convert the gas’s bulk kinetic energy relative to the suddenly stopped container into thermal internal energy.

**Assumptions**

- The gas is ideal with fixed molar mass M and constant gamma. The abrupt stop is followed by relaxation to rest relative to the vessel.
- The vessel is thermally insulated, rigid, and effectively held fixed during the subsequent gas relaxation. Its own heat capacity, deformation and energy absorption are neglected, so the gas bulk kinetic energy is retained as gas internal energy.

Immediately after an ideal abrupt stop, the gas still has bulk kinetic energy in the final vessel frame.

$$
K=\frac12mv^2,\qquad \Delta U=K.
$$

Express the heat capacity of the gas amount in terms of M and gamma, then equate its internal-energy rise to this kinetic energy.

$$
\Delta U=\frac mM\frac{R}{\gamma-1}\Delta T=\frac12mv^2.
$$

The gas mass cancels, leaving the temperature increment.

$$
\Delta T=\frac{(\gamma-1)Mv^2}{2R}.
$$

**Results**

Temperature increment after equilibration

$$
\Delta T=\frac{(\gamma-1)Mv^2}{2R}
$$

**Limitations**

- If the vessel walls share the deposited energy, or if the stopping mechanism removes a significant fraction of gas energy, the final gas rise also depends on those processes.

## 2.28

**Solved · high confidence.** Conserve total internal energy and total mole number when two insulated rigid air reservoirs equilibrate.

**Assumptions**

- Both gases are ideal air with the same molar heat capacity, taken constant over the temperatures involved.
- The vessels are rigid and thermally insulated as a combined system. The short connecting tube has negligible volume; transient kinetic energy vanishes in the final equilibrium.

Find the initial mole amounts from the known states.

$$
\nu_1=\frac{p_1V_1}{RT_1},\qquad\nu_2=\frac{p_2V_2}{RT_2}.
$$

No heat or work crosses the external boundary, so the initial and final total internal energies agree.

$$
\nu_1C_VT_1+\nu_2C_VT_2=(\nu_1+\nu_2)C_VT_f.
$$

Cancel the common heat capacity and insert the mole amounts to obtain the final temperature.

$$
T_f=\frac{\nu_1T_1+\nu_2T_2}{\nu_1+\nu_2}=\frac{p_1V_1+p_2V_2}{p_1V_1/T_1+p_2V_2/T_2}.
$$

Apply the ideal-gas law in the combined volume at the common final temperature.

$$
p_f=\frac{(\nu_1+\nu_2)RT_f}{V_1+V_2}=\frac{p_1V_1+p_2V_2}{V_1+V_2}.
$$

**Results**

Final equilibrium temperature

$$
T_f=\frac{p_1V_1+p_2V_2}{p_1V_1/T_1+p_2V_2/T_2}
$$

Final equilibrium pressure

$$
p_f=\frac{p_1V_1+p_2V_2}{V_1+V_2}
$$

**Limitations**

- Temperature-dependent heat capacities require conserving the corresponding internal-energy functions rather than a linear Cv T.

## 2.35

**Solved · high confidence.** Integrate the external lifting force after subtracting the upward gas force from the downward atmospheric force.

**Assumptions**

- The piston is weightless and frictionless, the gas contains one mole, and the isothermal expansion is quasistatic. Initially no applied lifting force is needed, so the initial gas pressure equals atmospheric pressure pa.
- The requested work is the work done by the external agent slowly raising the piston. The gas work and atmospheric work are listed separately to make the sign convention explicit. Let n=Vf/Vi>=1.

Force balance on the piston relates the applied upward force to atmospheric and gas pressures.

$$
F_{\rm lift}=(p_a-p)S,\qquad p=\frac{RT}{V},\qquad p_aV_i=RT.
$$

An upward piston displacement changes volume by dV=S dx, so the lifting work is the integral of the pressure difference.

$$
W_{\rm lift}=\int_{V_i}^{nV_i}\left(p_a-\frac{RT}{V}\right)dV=p_aV_i(n-1)-RT\ln n.
$$

Use the initial equilibrium relation to eliminate the initial volume and atmospheric pressure.

$$
W_{\rm lift}=RT\left(n-1-\ln n\right).
$$

The gas supplies part of the work against the atmosphere; the external agent supplies the rest.

$$
A_{\rm gas}=RT\ln n,\qquad W_{\rm against\ atmosphere}=RT(n-1),\qquad W_{\rm lift}+A_{\rm gas}=W_{\rm against\ atmosphere}.
$$

**Results**

Work performed by the agent raising the piston

$$
W_{\rm lift}=RT(n-1-\ln n)
$$

Work performed by the gas during the same expansion

$$
A_{\rm gas}=RT\ln n
$$

**Limitations**

- If the initial piston is already held by an applied force, its initial pressure need not equal pa; the more general work is pa Vi(n−1)−RT ln n.

## 2.38

**Solved · high confidence.** Eliminate volume or pressure between the ideal-gas law and the defining law of each process.

**Assumptions**

- A fixed amount of ideal gas has constant heat capacities, gamma=Cp/Cv>1. The plotted adiabatic process is quasistatic and reversible (isentropic).
- Use temperature on the horizontal axis. Normalize all four paths through a common reference state (p0,V0,T0), and write x=T/T0, y_p=p/p0, y_V=V/V0.

At fixed volume pressure is proportional to temperature, whereas at fixed pressure volume is proportional to temperature.

$$
\text{Isochore:}\quad y_p=x,\quad y_V=1;\qquad\text{isobar:}\quad y_p=1,\quad y_V=x.
$$

An isotherm has fixed horizontal coordinate on either diagram.

$$
\text{Isotherm:}\quad x=1\quad\text{(a vertical line in both panels).}
$$

For a reversible adiabat, pV^gamma is constant and TV^(gamma−1) is constant. Eliminate V in the first panel and solve for V in the second.

$$
\text{Adiabat:}\quad y_p=x^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)},\qquad y_V=x^{-1/(\gamma-1)},\quad x>0.
$$

These powers establish the curvature and the relative slopes through the common state.

$$
\left.\frac{dy_p}{dx}\right|_{x=1}=\frac\gamma{\gamma-1}>1,\qquad\left.\frac{dy_V}{dx}\right|_{x=1}=-\frac1{\gamma-1}<0.
$$

**Results**

Pressure–temperature curves

$$
y_p=x\ \text{(isochore)},\quad y_p=1\ \text{(isobar)},\quad x=1\ \text{(isotherm)},\quad y_p=x^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}\ \text{(reversible adiabat)}
$$

Volume–temperature curves

$$
y_V=1\ \text{(isochore)},\quad y_V=x\ \text{(isobar)},\quad x=1\ \text{(isotherm)},\quad y_V=x^{-1/(\gamma-1)}\ \text{(reversible adiabat)}
$$

**Limitations**

- An arbitrary irreversible adiabatic change has no universal state-space curve; the standard adiabatic diagrams require reversible quasistatic paths.

## 2.41

**Solved · high confidence.** Apply the first law to both gas compartments together while the conducting piston maintains a common temperature.

**Assumptions**

- The piston is frictionless, its weight and heat capacity are negligible, and the total cylinder volume Vc is fixed. An external force slowly displaces and holds it against the pressure difference.
- The same ideal gas is on both sides with constant gamma. Initially equal pressures, equal temperatures and equal volumes imply equal amounts, nu/2 on each side.
- Piston heat conduction and slow motion keep both gases at a common temperature T; the outside cylinder is insulated, so total external heat is zero. The two compartments are not individually adiabatic.

For volumes V1 and V2 at their common temperature, the total work performed by the gas pair is the sum of the two pressure-volume works.

$$
\delta W_{\rm by}=p_1dV_1+p_2dV_2=\frac{\nu RT}{2}\left(\frac{dV_1}{V_1}+\frac{dV_2}{V_2}\right)=\frac{\nu RT}{2}\,d\ln(V_1V_2).
$$

The total internal-energy change is supplied by external work, because external heat input vanishes.

$$
\nu C_VdT=-\delta W_{\rm by}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{dT}{T}=-\frac{\gamma-1}{2}\,d\ln(V_1V_2).
$$

Integrate from the initially equal-volume state.

$$
\frac{T}{T_0}=\left[\frac{(V_c/2)^2}{V_1V_2}\right]^{(\gamma-1)/2}.
$$

Express the volume product using eta=Vgreater/Vsmaller.

$$
V_{\rm small}=\frac{V_c}{1+\eta},\quad V_{\rm great}=\frac{\eta V_c}{1+\eta},\quad\frac{T}{T_0}=\left[\frac{(1+\eta)^2}{4\eta}\right]^{(\gamma-1)/2}=\left(\frac{1+\eta}{2\sqrt\eta}\right)^{\gamma-1},\quad\eta\geq1.
$$

**Results**

Common gas temperature at volume ratio eta

$$
T=T_0\left[\frac{(1+\eta)^2}{4\eta}\right]^{(\gamma-1)/2}
$$

**Limitations**

- Finite piston heat capacity or significant piston weight would require extra data and change the model. A free unforced piston at unequal compartment pressures would not remain at the specified displaced state.

## 2.57

**Solved · high confidence.** Integrate the one-mole van der Waals pressure along an isotherm.

**Assumptions**

- The expansion is quasistatic against a load matching the gas pressure. V1 and V2 denote the volume of one mole, with V2>V1>b and T>0.
- The constants a and b are temperature independent. The path follows the homogeneous van der Waals pressure where such a branch is physically admissible.

Insert the pressure equation into the work integral at fixed temperature.

$$
p=\frac{RT}{V-b}-\frac a{V^2},\qquad A=\int_{V_1}^{V_2}\left(\frac{RT}{V-b}-\frac a{V^2}\right)dV.
$$

Integrate the excluded-volume and attractive terms separately.

$$
A=RT\ln\frac{V_2-b}{V_1-b}+a\left(\frac1{V_2}-\frac1{V_1}\right).
$$

The corresponding internal-energy change provides a first-law check; it cancels the attractive contribution in the reversible heat.

$$
U=u(T)-\frac aV,\quad\Delta U=a\left(\frac1{V_1}-\frac1{V_2}\right),\qquad Q=\Delta U+A=RT\ln\frac{V_2-b}{V_1-b}.
$$

**Results**

Quasistatic isothermal work performed by one mole

$$
A=RT\ln\frac{V_2-b}{V_1-b}-a\left(\frac1{V_1}-\frac1{V_2}\right)
$$

**Limitations**

- This pressure integral describes quasistatic work. Expansion into vacuum has zero external work and is treated differently in Problems2.60–2.61.
- A real equilibrium path through two-phase coexistence requires the equilibrium isotherm rather than the unstable homogeneous van der Waals loop.

## 2.59

**Solved · high confidence.** Combine the van der Waals equation with the thermodynamic internal-energy identity, then evaluate reversible adiabats and heat-capacity differences.

**Assumptions**

- Let nu be the fixed mole number, V total volume, Vm=V/nu the molar volume, and a,b constant van der Waals parameters.
- Cv and Cp denote molar capacities. A thermodynamically compatible van der Waals caloric model has Cv=Cv(T), independent of volume; a constant Cv is an additional approximation, not a consequence of the pressure equation alone.
- The adiabatic curve means a reversible quasistatic adiabat, with deltaQ=0 and pressure work p dV. Use homogeneous states with T>0, Vm>b, positive pressure and negative isothermal pressure derivative.

The internal-energy identity fixes the volume dependence and leaves an arbitrary caloric function of temperature.

$$
p=\frac{\nu RT}{V-\nu b}-\frac{a\nu^2}{V^2},\qquad U=\nu u(T)-\frac{a\nu^2}{V},\qquad dU=\nu C_V(T)dT+\frac{a\nu^2}{V^2}dV.
$$

In a reversible adiabat the attractive contribution in dU cancels the attractive pressure work.

$$
0=dU+p\,dV=\nu C_V(T)dT+\frac{\nu RT}{V-\nu b}dV,\qquad\frac{C_V(T)}T dT+R\frac{dV}{V-\nu b}=0.
$$

Integrate in general without imposing constant heat capacity.

$$
\int_{T_*}^{T}\frac{C_V(\theta)}{\theta}\,d\theta+R\ln\frac{V-\nu b}{V_*-\nu b}=0.
$$

If Cv can be treated as constant, exponentiation gives the familiar power-law form using the available rather than total volume.

$$
\frac T{T_*}\left(\frac{V-\nu b}{V_*-\nu b}\right)^{R/C_V}=1,\qquad T(V_m-b)^{R/C_V}=\text{constant}.
$$

For the capacity difference use the thermodynamic derivative identity per mole.

$$
C_p-C_V=-T\frac{[(\partial p/\partial T)_{V_m}]^2}{(\partial p/\partial V_m)_T},\qquad\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_{V_m}=\frac R{V_m-b},\quad\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial V_m}\right)_T=-\frac{RT}{(V_m-b)^2}+\frac{2a}{V_m^3}.
$$

Substitution yields the requested function and displays its stable-branch denominator.

$$
C_p-C_V=\frac{R}{1-\dfrac{2a(V_m-b)^2}{RTV_m^3}}=\frac{R}{1-\dfrac{2a\nu(V-\nu b)^2}{RTV^3}}.\qquad1-\frac{2a(V_m-b)^2}{RTV_m^3}>0.
$$

**Results**

Reversible adiabat with general caloric heat capacity

$$
\int_{T_*}^{T}\frac{C_V(\theta)}{\theta}\,d\theta+R\ln\frac{V-\nu b}{V_*-\nu b}=0
$$

Adiabat when Cv is constant

$$
T(V-\nu b)^{R/C_V}=\text{constant}
$$

Difference of molar capacities

$$
C_p-C_V=\frac{R}{1-2a(V_m-b)^2/(RTV_m^3)}
$$

**Limitations**

- The equation of state alone does not determine Cv(T); the integral form is the general thermodynamic statement.
- Free expansion is adiabatic in the heat-transfer sense but irreversible and does not follow this reversible adiabatic curve.
- Two-phase coexistence and unstable homogeneous branches require a separate equilibrium treatment.
