## 2.160

**Solved · high confidence.** Apply the Laplace pressure jump with one interface for a droplet and two for a thin soap film.

**Assumptions**

- Both surfaces are spherical. Capillary pressure means internal minus external pressure, rather than absolute pressure.
- Mercury has one liquid-air interface. A thin soap bubble has inner and outer liquid-air interfaces with approximately the same radius.
- Appendix16 gives mercury surface tension490 mN/m=.490 N/m. The soap solution tension .045 N/m is supplied in the problem.

A spherical interface has two equal principal radii R=d/2.

$$
\Delta p_{\rm one}=\alpha(1/R+1/R)=2\alpha/R=4\alpha/d.
$$

Evaluate the mercury droplet pressure jump.

$$
\Delta p_a=4(0.490)/(1.5\times10^{-6})=1.30666667\times10^6\ {\rm Pa}.
$$

For the soap bubble, add the jumps across both sides of the thin liquid film.

$$
\Delta p_{\rm soap}=4\alpha/R=8\alpha/d,\qquad\Delta p_b=8(0.045)/(3.0\times10^{-3})=120\ {\rm Pa}.
$$

**Results**

Mercury droplet excess pressure

$$
\Delta p_a\simeq1.30667\ {\rm MPa}
$$

Soap bubble excess pressure

$$
\Delta p_b=120\ {\rm Pa}
$$

## 2.161

**Solved · high confidence.** Find the maximum curvature pressure a circular hole can support and balance the mercury hydrostatic head.

**Assumptions**

- The mercury free surface and the air below the hole are at the same atmospheric pressure. The upper free-surface curvature is negligible.
- The circular aperture is thin and its edge pins a single mercury-air meniscus. The largest attainable static pressure occurs for a hemispherical meniscus of radius d/2. This is the ideal maximum support, assuming edge geometry/contact-angle hysteresis allows it.
- Appendix11 gives mercury density13600 kg/m³ and Appendix16 tension.490 N/m; use rounded g=9.81 m/s².

The aperture radius fixes the smallest radius of curvature of a spanning spherical cap at the maximum-pressure configuration.

$$
R_{\min}=d/2,\qquad\Delta p_{\max}=2\alpha/R_{\min}=4\alpha/d.
$$

Balance the hydrostatic pressure at the hole against that capillary maximum.

$$
\rho gh_{\max}=4\alpha/d,\qquad h_{\max}=\frac{4\alpha}{\rho gd}.
$$

Evaluate the maximum mercury layer thickness.

$$
h_{\max}=\frac{4(0.490)}{13600(9.81)(70\times10^{-6})}=0.209869881\ {\rm m}\simeq21.0\ {\rm cm}.
$$

**Results**

Ideal maximum supported mercury layer thickness

$$
h_{\max}\simeq0.209870\ {\rm m}
$$

**Limitations**

- A thick bore, an unpinned edge or restricted contact angles can make the actual leaking threshold lower than this ideal maximum.

## 2.162

**Solved · high confidence.** Combine the two-interface soap-bubble pressure with conservation of isothermal trapped-gas content.

**Assumptions**

- The bubble has a thin film with two interfaces, constant tension alpha, fixed enclosed gas amount and the same temperature in both states. Gas dissolution and film-thickness volume are neglected.
- p0 is the initial external absolute air pressure. Final external pressure is p0/n and diameter is eta d, with n>1 and eta>1.

The initial and final enclosed-gas pressures each exceed their external pressures by the two-interface Laplace jump.

$$
p_i=p_0+8\alpha/d,\qquad p_f=p_0/n+8\alpha/(\eta d).
$$

Isothermal gas conservation makes the pressure-volume product constant; bubble volume increases by eta cubed.

$$
(p_0+8\alpha/d)d^3=[p_0/n+8\alpha/(\eta d)]\eta^3d^3.
$$

Collect the surface terms and solve for tension.

$$
\frac{8\alpha}{d}(\eta^2-1)=p_0(1-\eta^3/n),\qquad\alpha=\frac{p_0d(n-\eta^3)}{8n(\eta^2-1)}.
$$

**Results**

Soap solution surface tension

$$
\alpha=\frac{p_0d(n-\eta^3)}{8n(\eta^2-1)}
$$

**Limitations**

- No-change data n=eta=1 do not determine tension, and incompatible ratios would contradict the fixed-gas, constant-temperature film model.

## 2.166

**Solved · medium confidence.** Determine whether water reaches the open upper rim and use the hydrostatic head to find meniscus curvature.

**Assumptions**

## 2.167

**Solved · high confidence.** Balance the trapped-air compression against the wetting meniscus pressure when the two water levels coincide.

**Assumptions**

- The initially dry sealed glass tube contains air at the outside pressure p0 throughout length l. Its cross section is uniform; meniscus volume is negligible.
- Immersion is slow or followed by thermal equilibration, so trapped ideal air returns to its initial temperature and obeys an isothermal pressure-volume relation.

$$
p_l=p_0,\qquad p_g=p_0+4\alpha/d.

$$
p_0l=p_g(l-x),\qquad x=l\frac{4\alpha/d}{p_0+4\alpha/d}=\frac{4\alpha l}{p_0d+4\alpha}.

$$
4\alpha/d=14600\ {\rm Pa},\qquad x=0.110\frac{14600}{101300+14600}=0.0138567731\ {\rm m}.
$$

**Results**

Required submerged length with isothermal trapped air and complete wetting

$$
x\simeq13.8568\ {\rm mm}
$$

**Limitations**

## 2.168

**Solved · high confidence.** Combine the trapped-air compression, hydrostatic liquid pressure and signed meniscus pressure.

**Assumptions**

- Before the lower opening touches the liquid, trapped ideal gas fills tube length l at pressure p0. After the rise h, the trapped gas length is l−h, with0<h<l.
- The final and initial trapped-air temperatures are equal after equilibration, giving an isothermal pV relation. Cross section is constant and meniscus volume is neglected.
- The contact angle theta is measured through the liquid. A positive rise under this initial condition requires cos(theta)>0.

Isothermal trapped-air compression sets the gas pressure.

$$
p_g=p_0l/(l-h).
$$

The liquid rises h above the outside free surface, making its pressure below the meniscus less than p0.

$$
p_l=p_0-\rho gh.
$$

Subtract the two pressures and apply the spherical-cap wetting jump.

$$
p_g-p_l=\frac{p_0h}{l-h}+\rho gh=\frac{4\alpha\cos\theta}{d}.
$$

Solve for the surface tension.

$$
\alpha=\frac{dh}{4\cos\theta}\left(\rho g+\frac{p_0}{l-h}\right).
$$

**Results**

Surface tension inferred with isothermal trapped air

$$
\alpha=\frac{dh}{4\cos\theta}[\rho g+p_0/(l-h)]
$$

**Limitations**

- Without thermal equilibration, the trapped-gas law must be specified. For an ideal adiabatic compression replace p0*l/(l−h) by p0[l/(l−h)]^gamma before solving.

## 2.170

**Solved · high confidence.** Use the local parallel-plate Laplace pressure in the small-angle wedge and balance it against hydrostatic head.

**Assumptions**

- The wedge edge is vertical and delta_phi is a small positive angle in radians. At distance x from the edge the local gap is b(x)≈x delta_phi.
- The liquid wets the plates with contact angle theta<pi/2. Treat the meniscus locally as cylindrical across the narrow gap, neglecting its longitudinal curvature and edge-scale corrections.

The cross-gap meniscus radius gives the leading pressure depression.

$$
p_{\rm air}-p_{\rm liq}\simeq\frac{2\alpha\cos\theta}{b(x)},\qquad b(x)\simeq x\,\delta\varphi.
$$

The depression at height h equals rho g h relative to the reservoir surface.

$$
\rho gh(x)\simeq\frac{2\alpha\cos\theta}{x\,\delta\varphi}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad h(x)\simeq\frac{2\alpha\cos\theta}{\rho g\,\delta\varphi\,x}.
$$

**Results**

Leading capillary-rise profile in the narrow wedge

$$
h(x)\simeq\frac{2\alpha\cos\theta}{\rho g\,\delta\varphi\,x}
$$

**Limitations**

- The apparent divergence as x approaches zero is a continuum small-angle prediction, not a physical infinite-height answer at a microscopic or rounded edge. The approximation also excludes the near-neutral-wetting regime where neglected wedge and longitudinal curvature corrections can matter.

## 2.171

**Solved · high confidence.** Combine continuity and Bernoulli while retaining the cylindrical Laplace pressure of each jet section.

**Assumptions**

- The steady jet falls downward in air, is incompressible and inviscid, and remains axisymmetric and unbroken between the sections.
- Treat each section as locally cylindrical: the azimuthal curvature1/r dominates the much smaller axial curvature. Thus p−p_air=alpha/r=2alpha/d, with the same ambient air pressure at both heights.
- Use Appendix water alpha=.073 N/m and rho=1000 kg/m³; g=9.81 m/s². The lower diameter is d/n, with d=.0020 m, n=1.5 and height drop l=.020 m.

Continuity relates the speeds at the upper and lower sections.

$$
A_1=\pi d^2/4,\qquad A_2=A_1/n^2,\qquad v_2=n^2v_1,\qquad Q=A_1v_1.
$$

The smaller lower section has a larger positive internal Laplace pressure.

$$
p_1=p_a+\frac{2\alpha}{d},\qquad p_2=p_a+\frac{2\alpha n}{d},\qquad p_2-p_1=\frac{2\alpha(n-1)}d.
$$

Bernoulli allocates the gravitational head between acceleration and the increasing capillary pressure.

$$
\frac{v_2^2-v_1^2}{2}=gl-\frac{p_2-p_1}\rho\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_1^2=\frac{2gl-4\alpha(n-1)/(\rho d)}{n^4-1}.
$$

Insert the numerical data and compute discharge.

$$
v_1=0.280395325\ \mathrm{m/s},\quad v_2=0.630889482\ \mathrm{m/s},\qquad Q=\frac{\pi d^2}{4}\sqrt{\frac{2gl-4\alpha(n-1)/(\rho d)}{n^4-1}}=8.80887894\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^3/s}.
$$

**Results**

Volume flow rate including the cylindrical capillary-pressure correction

$$
Q=\frac{\pi d^2}{4}\sqrt{\frac{2gl-4\alpha(n-1)/(\rho d)}{n^4-1}}\simeq0.881\ \mathrm{cm^3/s}
$$

Reference value if surface tension were neglected

$$
Q_{\alpha=0}=\frac{\pi d^2}{4}\sqrt{\frac{2gl}{n^4-1}}\simeq0.976\ \mathrm{cm^3/s}
$$

**Limitations**

- Viscosity, axial surface curvature, external aerodynamic pressure variation or a nonuniform speed profile would change this ideal steady slender-jet model.

## 2.172

**Provisional · low confidence.** Separate the literal radius-difference question from the conditional inverse-curvature relation and weak-deformation estimate.

**Assumptions**

- The scan asks for the difference of curvature radii, not the difference of their reciprocals. Height h alone does not specify two individual surface radii.
- At axisymmetric upper and lower poles each pair of principal radii is equal; call their positive convex values Rt and Rb. An exact normal-stress balance also involves the exterior aerodynamic and interior viscous normal stresses.
- Only for the conventional auxiliary model of hydrostatic interior pressure and a common exterior pressure, neglecting other normal stresses, may one set p_liq,b−p_liq,t=rho g h and use the spherical-pole Young–Laplace jump2alpha/R.
- The numerical radius difference below additionally assumes weak deformation about a sphere of radius h/2. It is a conditional estimate, not a uniquely determined solution for a terminally falling drop.

Let tau_nn denote viscous normal stress and take the interface normal from liquid to air. The exact constant-tension normal-traction jump at an axisymmetric pole is 2alpha/R=Pi, where Pi includes both pressures and viscous stresses. Subtracting the pole equations retains the missing terminal-flow data.

$$
\Pi=p_{\rm liq}-p_{\rm air}+\tau_{{\rm air},nn}-\tau_{{\rm liq},nn},\qquad\frac{2\alpha}{R}=\Pi,\qquad2\alpha\left(\frac1{R_b}-\frac1{R_t}\right)=\Pi_b-\Pi_t.
$$

With the additional hydrostatic and uniform-exterior idealizations, the known height fixes only the reciprocal-radius difference.

$$
\frac1{R_b}-\frac1{R_t}=\frac{\rho gh}{2\alpha}=154.541096\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}.
$$

The actual radius difference still contains the unknown product of the radii.

$$
R_t-R_b=\frac{\rho gh}{2\alpha}R_tR_b.\qquad\text{The supplied }h\text{ does not fix }R_tR_b.
$$

A nearly spherical approximation replaces that product by (h/2)² and produces the conventional estimate.

$$
R_t\simeq R_b\simeq h/2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad R_t-R_b\simeq\frac{\rho gh^3}{8\alpha}=2.04380599\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm m=0.204381\ \mathrm{mm}.
$$

The corresponding Bond number quantifies the small-deformation assumption but supplies no missing aerodynamic boundary data.

$$
\mathrm{Bo}=\frac{\rho g(h/2)^2}{\alpha}=0.17772226.
$$

**Results**

Literal radius difference under the supplied data

$$
R_t-R_b\ \text{is not uniquely determined by }h\text{ and terminal motion alone}
$$

Additional shape information and moving-drop stress data are needed.

Inverse-radius difference under the auxiliary hydrostatic/uniform-exterior model

$$
R_b^{-1}-R_t^{-1}=\rho gh/(2\alpha)\simeq154.54\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}
$$

Additional weak-deformation radius-difference estimate

$$
R_t-R_b\simeq\rho gh^3/(8\alpha)\simeq0.204\ \mathrm{mm}
$$

Requires Rt Rb≈h²/4 in addition to the auxiliary stress model; not an exact or unique terminal-drop answer.

**Limitations**

- Terminal speed means zero translational acceleration, not zero gravity. Aerodynamic and possibly viscous stresses are required to support the drop weight and are not specified.
- A hydrostatic interior and uniform exterior pressure with surface tension alone cannot provide global support for a free heavy drop; their use above is only a local conventional simplification.
- Even after that simplification, only the inverse-radius difference is determined. The source wording is therefore not silently changed to make a unique exact answer.

## 2.173

**Solved · high confidence.** Conserve the mercury volume and compare the initial and final upward capillary pressure forces on the upper plate.

**Assumptions**

- The mercury tablet is thin, h much smaller than R, with a circular footprint. Its volume is approximately pi R²h, and the plates are large enough for the footprint to expand.
- Use the same equilibrium contact angle before and after loading, measured through mercury; the numerical angle 135° is nonwetting. Ignore contact-angle hysteresis, line-force and azimuthal-curvature terms of relative order h/R, and hydrostatic variation across the thin drop.
- The upper plate is initially in vertical balance under its existing downward load. The requested mass is an added load increment; any pre-existing plate weight remains unchanged.
- Ambient pressure is the same around the bridge and on the exterior plate face. Appendix mercury surface tension is .490 N/m; g=9.81 m/s².

The dominant cross-gap meniscus curvature produces a positive mercury overpressure for theta>90°.

$$
\Delta p=p_{Hg}-p_a\simeq-\frac{2\alpha\cos\theta}{h}>0,\qquad F_0=\pi R^2\Delta p=-\frac{2\pi R^2\alpha\cos\theta}{h}.
$$

Reducing the gap by n increases footprint area by n and pressure by n.

$$
h_f=h/n,\quad A_f=n\pi R^2,\quad R_f=\sqrt n R,\quad\Delta p_f=n\Delta p,\quad F_f=n^2F_0.
$$

Subtract the pre-existing balanced load instead of equating the added weight to the whole final force.

$$
mg=F_f-F_0=(n^2-1)F_0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad m=-\frac{2\pi R^2\alpha\cos\theta}{gh}(n^2-1).
$$

Insert the supplied geometry, contact angle and n=2.

$$
F_0=2.29159225\ \mathrm N,\qquad m=\frac{3F_0}{9.81}=0.700792738\ \mathrm{kg}\simeq0.70\ \mathrm{kg}.
$$

**Results**

Added mass required to reduce the gap n-fold

$$
m=\frac{2\pi R^2\alpha(-\cos\theta)}{gh}(n^2-1)\simeq0.701\ \mathrm{kg}
$$

**Limitations**

- If the initial gap is fixed by a spacer or an unreported holding force rather than an initially balanced load, the absolute final applied mass also depends on that initial support. The formula gives the added load under the stated initial-balance convention.

## 2.176

**Solved · high confidence.** Integrate the hydrostatic pressure deficit above the reservoir surface over the capillary-rise strip.

**Assumptions**

- The plates are parallel, with gap d much smaller than width l. Complete wetting and negligible side-edge menisci give a cylindrical cross-gap meniscus.
- The reservoir is large and the capillary column stays below the upper plate edges as stated. Outside each plate the liquid below reservoir level and air above it have the ordinary hydrostatic pressures.
- The force quoted is the attraction on either one plate, not the sum of the two equal and opposite force magnitudes. Use Appendix water alpha=.073 N/m and rho=1000 kg/m³, with g=9.81 m/s².

The Laplace depression at the top balances the hydrostatic rise height.

$$
H=\frac{2\alpha}{\rho gd}=\frac{2(0.073)}{(1000)(9.81)(10^{-4})}=0.148827727\ \mathrm m.
$$

Above reservoir level, at elevation z, the inside water pressure is lower than exterior air pressure by rho g z. Below reservoir level both faces are in water at equal hydrostatic pressure.

$$
p_{out}-p_{in}=\rho gz\quad(0<z<H),\qquad dF=l\rho gz\,dz.
$$

Integrate this triangular pressure distribution, rather than applying the top depression uniformly over the whole column.

$$

## 2.177

**Solved · medium confidence.** Couple the two-interface soap-bubble overpressure to capillary Poiseuille outflow and the shrinking bubble volume.

**Assumptions**

- Write a(t) for the instantaneous bubble radius, with a(0)=R; r is the fixed internal radius of the capillary. The bubble is spherical and has two liquid-gas interfaces, so its Laplace overpressure is4alpha/a.

$$
\Delta p=\frac{4\alpha}{a},\qquad Q=\frac{\pi r^4}{8\eta l}\Delta p=\frac{\pi\alpha r^4}{2\eta l a}.
$$

$$

$$

$$

$$
\tau_{\rm iso}=\frac{8\eta l}{\alpha r^4}\int_{a_f}^{R}a^3\frac{p_0a+8\alpha/3}{p_0a+2\alpha}\,da;\qquad p_0a\gg\alpha:\quad\tau_{\rm iso}\to\frac{2\eta l}{\alpha r^4}(R^4-a_f^4).

Lifetime in the low-overpressure Poiseuille approximation

$$
\tau\simeq\frac{2\eta l}{\alpha}\left(\frac Rr\right)^4
$$

$$

**Solved · medium confidence.** Balance the wetting surface-free-energy decrease against gravitational storage and dissipation, and separate dissipation from total isothermal heat.

**Assumptions**

- Let b be the capillary internal radius, h the final rise, and rho the water density. Use a wide reservoir with negligible surface-level change, complete wetting in the classical Young model, and a thin capillary with h much greater than b.
- Neglect meniscus end-area and volume corrections, evaporation, solid heat capacity changes and initial/final liquid kinetic energy. The water settles to equilibrium after viscous damping.
- For the conventional heat result neglect the temperature dependence of interfacial free energies. A complete isothermal thermal accounting additionally needs alpha prime=dalpha/dT and the absolute temperature, neither of which is specified.

The final capillary rise is determined by surface force supporting the water column.

$$
2\pi b\alpha=\rho\pi b^2gh,\qquad h=\frac{2\alpha}{\rho gb}.
$$

Complete wetting lowers the wall interfacial free energy by alpha per wetted area. Compare that released free energy with the gravitational energy retained in the column.

$$
A_w=2\pi bh,\qquad-\Delta F_{\rm wet}=\alpha A_w,\qquad E_g=\frac12\rho\pi b^2gh^2=\frac12\alpha A_w.
$$

The remaining mechanical free energy is dissipated on settling. Eliminating h makes it independent of capillary radius.

$$
D=\alpha A_w-E_g=E_g=\frac{2\pi\alpha^2}{\rho g}.
$$

For full isothermal heat accounting, use the wetting surface entropy and internal-energy changes before adding gravity.

$$
\Delta F_{\rm wet}=-\alpha A_w,\quad\Delta S_{\rm wet}=\alpha^{\prime}A_w,\quad\Delta U_{\rm wet}=-(\alpha-T\alpha^{\prime})A_w,\qquad Q_{\rm out}=-[\Delta U_{\rm wet}+E_g].

This extra reversible thermal term is not inferable from the supplied alpha alone.

$$
Q_{\rm out}=D-T\alpha^{\prime}A_w=\frac{2\pi\alpha^2}{\rho g}-\frac{4\pi T\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}{\rho g}=\frac{2\pi\alpha}{\rho g}(\alpha-2T\alpha^{\prime}).
$$

**Results**

Conventional liberated dissipative energy when interfacial entropy is neglected

$$
Q_{\rm conventional}=D=\frac{2\pi\alpha^2}{\rho g}
$$

Total isothermal heat released with an interfacial temperature coefficient

$$
Q_{\rm out}=\frac{2\pi\alpha}{\rho g}(\alpha-2T\alpha^{\prime})
$$

Uses the Young complete-wetting free-energy model. Alpha prime and temperature are absent from the problem, so this thermal quantity is not uniquely fixed by the given alpha alone.

**Limitations**

- The full isothermal expression assumes the Young complete-wetting interfacial free-energy difference is−alpha. Other wetting-film thermodynamics would need solid-liquid/solid-vapor interfacial data.
- The statement supplies no temperature coefficient or absolute temperature. Its alpha-only answer therefore refers to the conventional dissipation approximation.

## 2.183

**Solved · high confidence.** Apply the efficiency of an infinitesimal reversible Carnot cycle in temperature and physical surface area.

**Assumptions**

- Alpha is a differentiable function of temperature for the surface at the specified composition. Let A be total physical liquid-gas interface area, counting each face separately.
- The reversible mechanical work done on the surface is alpha dA. Let q(T) be reversible isothermal heat absorbed per increment of physical area.

Stretch at hot temperature T and contract at the slightly colder temperature T−dT, with reversible adiabatic connectors. The generalized-work loop area gives the mechanical work output.

$$
\delta W_{\rm out}=[\alpha(T-dT)-\alpha(T)]dA=-\alpha^{\prime}(T)dT\,dA.
$$

Carnot efficiency equates work divided by hot isothermal heat to dT/T.

$$
\delta Q_h=q(T)dA,\qquad\frac{\delta W_{\rm out}}{\delta Q_h}=\frac{dT}{T}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad q(T)=-T\frac{d\alpha}{dT}.
$$

The free-energy identity independently checks the sign and physical-area convention.

$$
F_s=\alpha(T)A,\quad S_s=-\left(\frac{\partial F_s}{\partial T}\right)_A=-A\alpha^{\prime}(T),\quad T\left(\frac{\partial S_s}{\partial A}\right)_T=-T\alpha^{\prime}(T)=q.
$$

**Results**

Reversible heat absorbed to form unit physical interface area isothermally

$$
q=-T\,d\alpha/dT
$$

**Limitations**

- For the usual engine orientation in the Carnot argument alpha prime is negative. With the opposite slope reverse the orientation; the signed heat identity remains the same.
