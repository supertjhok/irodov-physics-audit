## 1.234

**Problem statement**

A thin uniform rod AB of mass m = 1.0 kg moves translationally with acceleration w = 2.0 m/s² due to two antiparallel forces F_1 and F_2 (Fig. 1.52). The distance between the points at which these forces are applied is equal to a = 20 cm. Besides, it is known that F_2 = 5.0 N. Find the length of the rod.

**Solved · high confidence.** Combine zero torque about the centre of mass with the translational equation.

**Assumptions**

- The rod is uniform; its centre of mass is its midpoint. The forces have the application points and directions in Fig. 1.52.

In Fig. 1.52, F1 acts leftward at A and F2 acts rightward a below A. Write L for the rod length. Vanishing angular acceleration requires zero net torque about the midpoint.

$$
F_1L/2-F_2(L/2-a)=0.
$$

The torque equation gives a positive rightward resultant, so its magnitude is mw. Eliminate F1 to find L.

$$
F_2-F_1=mw,\qquad mwL/2=F_2a,\qquad L=\frac{2F_2a}{mw}.
$$

Convert the given distance to metres and substitute the data.

$$
L=\frac{2(5.0)(0.20)}{(1.0)(2.0)}=1.0\ \mathrm m,\qquad F_1=3.0\ \mathrm N.
$$

**Results**

Rod length

$$
L=1.0\ \mathrm m
$$

## 1.235

**Problem statement**

A force F = Ai + Bj is applied to a point whose radius vector relative to the origin of coordinates O is equal to $\mathbf{r}=a\mathbf{i}+b\mathbf{j}$, where a, b, A, B are constants, and i, j are the unit vectors of the x and y axes. Find the moment N and the arm l of the force F relative to the point O.

**Solved · high confidence.** Use the cross product and divide its magnitude by the force magnitude.

The moment of a force about the origin is the cross product of its position vector and the force.

$$
\mathbf N=\mathbf r\times\mathbf F=(aB-bA)\mathbf k.
$$

The arm is the shortest distance between O and the force line of action. Its product with the force magnitude equals the torque magnitude.

$$
l=\frac{|\mathbf r\times\mathbf F|}{|\mathbf F|}=\frac{|aB-bA|}{\sqrt{A^2+B^2}}\quad(A^2+B^2>0).
$$

**Results**

Torque vector

$$
\mathbf N=(aB-bA)\mathbf k
$$

Perpendicular force arm

$$
l=\frac{|aB-bA|}{\sqrt{A^2+B^2}}
$$

**Limitations**

- For A=B=0 the torque is zero, but the line of action and its arm are undefined.

## 1.236

**Problem statement**

A force $\mathbf{F}_1=A\mathbf{j}$ is applied to a point whose radius vector $\mathbf{r}_1=a\mathbf{i}$, while a force $\mathbf{F}_2=B\mathbf{i}$ is applied to the point whose radius vector $\mathbf{r}_2=b\mathbf{j}$. Both radius vectors are determined relative to the origin of coordinates O, i and j are the unit vectors of the x and y axes, a, b, A, B are constants. Find the arm l of the resultant force relative to the point O.

**Solved · high confidence.** Replace the two forces by their resultant while preserving the moment about O.

Add the two orthogonal forces and their moments about O, retaining their signs.

$$
\mathbf F=B\mathbf i+A\mathbf j,\qquad \mathbf N=(aA-bB)\mathbf k.
$$

A single resultant placed on a suitable line has the same torque. Its perpendicular arm is torque magnitude divided by resultant magnitude.

$$
l=\frac{|aA-bB|}{\sqrt{A^2+B^2}}.
$$

For example, a point on that resultant line can be chosen perpendicular to F; direct substitution verifies its moment.

$$
\mathbf r_0=\frac{aA-bB}{A^2+B^2}(A\mathbf i-B\mathbf j),\qquad \mathbf r_0\times\mathbf F=(aA-bB)\mathbf k.
$$

**Results**

Arm of the resultant about O

$$
l=\frac{|aA-bB|}{\sqrt{A^2+B^2}}
$$

**Limitations**

- The formula assumes a nonzero resultant. If A=B=0 both forces vanish and the arm has no definition.

## 1.237

**Problem statement**

Three forces are applied to a square plate as shown in Fig. 1.53. Find the modulus, direction, and the point of application of the resultant force, if this point is taken on the side BC.

**Solved · high confidence.** Sum force vectors and torques using coordinates along the square diagonals.

**Assumptions**

- The force magnitudes and arrows are read from Fig. 1.53: upward F at A, rightward F at B, and down-right F√2 at D.

Choose the square centre as origin with x along AC toward C and y along DB toward B. Let d be half a diagonal.

$$
A=(-d,0),\ B=(0,d),\ C=(d,0),\ D=(0,-d).
$$

Resolve the oblique force along x and y and sum the three forces.

$$
\mathbf F_A=(0,F),\quad\mathbf F_B=(F,0),\quad\mathbf F_D=(F,-F),\quad\mathbf F_{\rm res}=(2F,0).
$$

The three moments about the centre determine the height of the horizontal resultant.

$$
N_z=-dF-dF+dF=-dF=-2Fy,\qquad y=d/2.
$$

The side BC satisfies x+y=d. Hence the resultant intersects BC at its midpoint.

$$
x=y=d/2,\qquad BP=PC=BC/2.
$$

**Results**

Resultant magnitude

$$
F_{\rm res}=2F
$$

Resultant direction

$$
\mathbf F_{\rm res}\parallel\overrightarrow{AC}
$$

Application point on BC

$$
P=(B+C)/2
$$

The midpoint of BC; the arrow points from A toward C, parallel to AC.

## 1.238

**Problem statement**

Find the moment of inertia (a) of a thin uniform rod relative to the axis which is perpendicular to the rod and passes through its end, if the mass of the rod is m and its length l; (b) of a thin uniform rectangular plate relative to the axis passing perpendicular to the plane of the plate through one of its vertices, if the sides of the plate are equal to a and b, and its mass is m.

**Solved · high confidence.** Integrate the squared distance from the specified perpendicular axis over a uniform mass distribution.

For the rod, measure x from the end through which the axis passes and use the linear density m/l.

$$
I_{\rm rod}=\frac ml\int_0^l x^2\,dx=\frac13ml^2.
$$

For the plate, choose the vertex as the origin. The distance from the perpendicular axis is sqrt(x²+y²).

$$
I_{\rm plate}=\frac m{ab}\int_0^a\!\int_0^b(x^2+y^2)\,dy\,dx=\frac m3(a^2+b^2).
$$

**Results**

Rod about perpendicular end axis

$$
I=\frac13ml^2
$$

Rectangular plate about perpendicular vertex axis

$$
I=\frac m3(a^2+b^2)
$$

## 1.239

**Problem statement**

Calculate the moment of inertia (a) of a copper uniform disc relative to the symmetry axis perpendicular to the plane of the disc, if its thickness is equal to b=2.0 mm and its radius to R = 100 mm; (b) of a uniform solid cone relative to its symmetry axis, if the mass of the cone is equal to m and the radius of its base to R.

**Solved · high confidence.** Integrate concentric annuli for the disc and coaxial thin discs for the cone.

**Assumptions**

- For part (a), use the book Appendix Table 11 copper density ρ=8.9 g/cm³=8.90×10³ kg/m³ (PDF381, printed374).

An annulus in the copper disc has mass 2πρbr dr. Integrating its contribution r² dm gives the axial inertia.

$$
I_{\rm disc}=2\pi\rho b\int_0^Rr^3\,dr=\frac12\pi\rho bR^4=\frac12MR^2,\quad M=\pi\rho bR^2.
$$

In SI units the copper disc mass is approximately 0.5592 kg and its axial inertia is approximately 0.002796 kg m².

$$
b=0.0020\ \mathrm m,\quad R=0.100\ \mathrm m,\quad I=2.7960\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\simeq2.8\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}.
$$

For the cone let z run from the apex to the base at height H. A slice has radius Rz/H and contributes half its mass times the squared slice radius.

$$
dm=\rho\pi(Rz/H)^2dz,\qquad I=\frac12\rho\pi\frac{R^4}{H^4}\int_0^Hz^4dz=\frac{\rho\pi R^4H}{10}.
$$

Use the cone mass to eliminate both its unknown height and material density.

$$
m=\frac13\rho\pi R^2H,\qquad I_{\rm cone}=\frac3{10}mR^2.
$$

**Results**

Copper disc axial moment of inertia

$$
I\simeq2.8\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}
$$

Computed with copper density from Appendix Table 11, PDF381 (printed374).

Solid cone axial moment of inertia

$$
I=\frac3{10}mR^2
$$

**Limitations**

- The density is the rounded tabular value; the symbolic inertia retains the density dependence.

## 1.240

**Problem statement**

Demonstrate that in the case of a thin plate of arbitrary shape there is the following relationship between the moments of inertia: I_1 + I_2 = I_3, where subindices 1, 2, and 3 define three mutually perpendicular axes passing through one point, with axes 1 and 2 lying in the plane of the plate. Using this relationship, find the moment of inertia of a thin uniform round disc of radius R and mass m relative to the axis coinciding with one of its diameters.

**Solved · high confidence.** Prove the perpendicular-axis identity pointwise and apply rotational symmetry.

**Assumptions**

- The plate thickness is negligible, so every mass element lies in the plane of axes 1 and 2.

Place the common axis intersection at the origin and let axes 1,2 be x,y in the plate plane. A point (x,y,0) has squared distances y², x², and x²+y² to the three axes.

$$
I_1=\int y^2\,dm,\qquad I_2=\int x^2\,dm,\qquad I_3=\int(x^2+y^2)\,dm.
$$

Adding the first two integrals proves the theorem without requiring the origin to be the centre of mass.

$$
I_1+I_2=I_3.
$$

For a uniform circular disc, its two perpendicular diameters are equivalent by symmetry; its normal central inertia is mR²/2.

$$
I_1=I_2,\qquad 2I_{\rm diameter}=\frac12mR^2,\qquad I_{\rm diameter}=\frac14mR^2.
$$

**Results**

Perpendicular-axis relation

$$
I_1+I_2=I_3
$$

Disc moment about a diameter

$$
I_{\rm diameter}=\frac14mR^2
$$

## 1.241

**Problem statement**

A uniform disc of radius R = 20 cm has a round cut as shown in Fig. 1.54. The mass of the remaining (shaded) portion of the disc equals m = 7.3 kg. Find the moment of inertia of such a disc relative to the axis passing through its centre of inertia and perpendicular to the plane of the disc.

**Solved · high confidence.** Subtract the removed disc and then translate the axis to the remaining centre of mass.

**Assumptions**

- Fig. 1.54 shows a hole whose circumference passes through the original disc centre O and is internally tangent to the outer circumference; its radius is R/2 and its centre is R/2 from O. The given m is the remaining mass.

Since the hole has one quarter of the original area, write the original and removed masses in terms of the remaining mass m.

$$
M_0=\frac43m,\qquad m_h=\frac13m,\qquad r_h=R/2.
$$

Take the hole centre at x=−R/2. Subtracting its first mass moment shifts the remaining centre of mass to the opposite side of O.

$$
mx_C=M_0(0)-m_h(-R/2),\qquad x_C=R/6.
$$

Subtract the hole inertia about O, including its parallel-axis term.

$$
I_O=\frac12M_0R^2-\left[\frac12m_h(R/2)^2+m_h(R/2)^2\right]=\frac{13}{24}mR^2.
$$

Translate from O to the remaining centre of mass. The removed parallel-axis contribution is based on the remaining mass.

$$
I_C=I_O-m(R/6)^2=\left(\frac{13}{24}-\frac1{36}\right)mR^2=\frac{37}{72}mR^2.
$$

Insert the given SI data.

$$
I_C=\frac{37}{72}(7.3)(0.20)^2=0.1500556\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\simeq0.15\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}.
$$

**Results**

Moment of inertia about the remaining centre of mass

$$
I_C=\frac{37}{72}mR^2\simeq0.15\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}
$$

Remaining centre-of-mass displacement

$$
OC=R/6
$$

Away from the circular hole, along the line of centres.

## 1.242

**Problem statement**

Using the formula for the moment of inertia of a uniform sphere, find the moment of inertia of a thin spherical layer of mass m and radius R relative to the axis passing through its centre.

**Solved · high confidence.** Differentiate the uniform solid-sphere inertia with respect to its radius.

**Assumptions**

- The thin spherical layer is a uniform shell with negligible radial thickness.

Consider a family of solid uniform spheres of density ρ. Increasing the radius by dR adds only a thin spherical shell.

$$
M(R)=\frac{4\pi\rho}{3}R^3,\qquad I(R)=\frac25M(R)R^2=\frac{8\pi\rho}{15}R^5.
$$

The infinitesimal shell mass and moment of inertia are the derivatives of these two functions times dR.

$$
dM=4\pi\rho R^2dR,\qquad dI=\frac{8\pi\rho}{3}R^4dR,\qquad \frac{dI}{dM}=\frac23R^2.
$$

Replace the infinitesimal shell mass by the specified thin-shell mass m.

$$
I_{\rm shell}=\frac23mR^2.
$$

**Results**

Thin spherical-shell central moment of inertia

$$
I=\frac23mR^2
$$

## 1.243

**Problem statement**

A light thread with a body of mass m tied to its end is wound on a uniform solid cylinder of mass M and radius R (Fig. 1.55). At a moment t = 0 the system is set in motion. Assuming the friction in the axle of the cylinder to be negligible, find the time dependence of (a) the angular velocity of the cylinder; (b) the kinetic energy of the whole system.

**Solved · high confidence.** Solve the coupled translation and rotation equations and use the no-slip constraint.

**Assumptions**

- The massless thread remains taut and unwinds without slipping. The axle is fixed and frictionless. The stated start at t=0 is interpreted as release from rest.

Let a be the downward acceleration of the hanging mass, T the tension, and β the unwinding angular acceleration. The cylinder inertia is MR²/2.

$$
mg-T=ma,\qquad TR=\frac12MR^2\beta,\qquad a=R\beta.
$$

Eliminating T gives a constant acceleration and hence the angular velocity from rest.

$$
a=\frac{mg}{m+M/2},\qquad \omega(t)=\frac{mgt}{R(m+M/2)}.
$$

Add the translating mass energy and the rotating cylinder energy, with v=Rω=at.

$$
K=\frac12mv^2+\frac12\left(\frac12MR^2\right)\omega^2=\frac12(m+M/2)a^2t^2=\frac{m^2g^2t^2}{2m+M}.
$$

**Results**

Cylinder angular velocity after release

$$
\omega(t)=\frac{mgt}{R(m+M/2)}
$$

Positive in the unwinding sense.

Total kinetic energy after release

$$
K(t)=\frac{m^2g^2t^2}{2m+M}
$$

**Limitations**

- If set in motion means a nonzero initial speed v0 instead of release from rest, replace at by v0+at: ω=(v0+at)/R and K=(m+M/2)(v0+at)²/2. The statement supplies no v0.

## 1.244

**Problem statement**

The ends of thin threads tightly wound on the axle of radius r of the Maxwell disc are attached to a horizontal bar. When the disc unwinds, the bar is raised to keep the disc at the same height. The mass of the disc with the axle is equal to m, the moment of inertia of the arrangement relative to its axis is I. Find the tension of each thread and the acceleration of the bar.

**Solved · high confidence.** Use vertical equilibrium of the stationary disc centre and the moving-support no-slip constraint.

**Assumptions**

- The two threads are identical, massless, taut, symmetrically wound, and do not slip on the axle. The disc centre remains at a fixed height.

Since the disc has zero vertical acceleration, its weight is supported equally by the two threads.

$$
2T-mg=0,\qquad T=mg/2.
$$

Both thread tensions produce torques in the unwinding sense. Thus the disc has a constant angular acceleration.

$$
I\beta=2Tr=mgr,\qquad \beta=\frac{mgr}{I}.
$$

A bar displacement raises the free thread ends by the length unwound while the centre remains stationary. The bar speed is rω and its upward acceleration is rβ.

$$
y_{\rm bar}-y_{\rm bar,0}=r\varphi,\qquad a_{\rm bar}=r\beta=\frac{mgr^2}{I}.
$$

**Results**

Tension of each thread

$$
T=\frac12mg
$$

Upward acceleration of the bar

$$
a_{\rm bar}=\frac{mgr^2}{I}
$$

## 1.245

**Problem statement**

A thin horizontal uniform rod AB of mass m and length l can rotate freely about a vertical axis passing through its end A. At a certain moment the end B starts experiencing a constant force F which is always perpendicular to the original position of the stationary rod and directed in a horizontal plane. Find the angular velocity of the rod as a function of its rotation angle φ counted relative to the initial position.

**Solved · high confidence.** Integrate the work of the fixed laboratory-direction force over the rod end displacement.

**Assumptions**

- The rod starts from rest at φ=0. Choose positive φ toward the applied force. The force has fixed direction perpendicular to the initial rod, rather than to its instantaneous direction.

Put the initial rod along x and the force along +y. At angle φ the free end has y=l sinφ, so the work from its initial position is Fl sinφ.

$$
W=\int_0^{l\sin\varphi}F\,dy=Fl\sin\varphi.
$$

The vertical end-axis moment of inertia is ml²/3, and work equals rotational kinetic energy.

$$
\frac12\left(\frac13ml^2\right)\omega^2=Fl\sin\varphi,\qquad \omega^2=\frac{6F}{ml}\sin\varphi.
$$

Initially the rod moves toward increasing φ. It reaches π with zero speed and then reverses; the same energy expression holds with the opposite velocity sign on the returning branch.

$$
\omega(\varphi)=\begin{cases}+\sqrt{\dfrac{6F\sin\varphi}{ml}},&\text{outgoing},\\-\sqrt{\dfrac{6F\sin\varphi}{ml}},&\text{returning},\end{cases}\qquad0\le\varphi\le\pi.
$$

**Results**

Angular speed as a function of rotation angle

$$
|\omega(\varphi)|=\sqrt{\frac{6F}{ml}\sin\varphi},\qquad0\le\varphi\le\pi
$$

The angular velocity is positive on the first outgoing passage and negative on the return; it is zero at the endpoints.

## 1.246

**Problem statement**

In the arrangement shown in Fig. 1.56 the mass of the uniform solid cylinder of radius R is equal to m and the masses of two bodies are equal to m_1 and m_2 . The thread slipping and the friction in the axle of the cylinder are supposed to be absent. Find the angular acceleration of the cylinder and the ratio of tensions T_1/T_2 of the vertical sections of the thread in the process of motion.

**Solved · high confidence.** Write the two hanging-mass equations and the massive-pulley torque equation.

**Assumptions**

- The thread is massless, taut, and does not slip. The axle is frictionless. Define positive acceleration a as m2 moving down and m1 moving up.

The tensions need not be equal because their difference accelerates the massive pulley.

$$
T_1-m_1g=m_1a,\quad m_2g-T_2=m_2a,\quad (T_2-T_1)R=\frac12mR^2\frac aR.
$$

Adding these equations and setting D=m1+m2+m/2 gives the signed acceleration.

$$
D=m_1+m_2+m/2,\qquad a=\frac{(m_2-m_1)g}{D},\qquad\beta=\frac{(m_2-m_1)g}{RD}.
$$

Substitute a into each mass equation to find both tensions and their ratio.

$$
T_1=\frac{m_1g(2m_2+m/2)}D,\qquad T_2=\frac{m_2g(2m_1+m/2)}D,\qquad\frac{T_1}{T_2}=\frac{m_1(4m_2+m)}{m_2(4m_1+m)}.
$$

**Results**

Signed angular acceleration of cylinder

$$
\beta=\frac{(m_2-m_1)g}{R(m_1+m_2+m/2)}
$$

Positive when m2 descends; reverse the sign convention if m1 is the descending mass. The magnitude uses |m2−m1|.

Ratio of vertical thread tensions

$$
\frac{T_1}{T_2}=\frac{m_1(4m_2+m)}{m_2(4m_1+m)}
$$

## 1.247

**Problem statement**

In the system shown in Fig. 1.57 the masses of the bodies are known to be m_1 and m_2 , the coefficient of friction between the body m_1 and the horizontal plane is equal to k, and a pulley of mass m is assumed to be a uniform disc. The thread does not slip over the pulley. At the moment t = 0 the body m_2 starts descending. Assuming the mass of the thread and the friction in the axle of the pulley to be negligible, find the work performed by the friction forces acting on the body m_1 over the first t seconds after the beginning of motion.

**Solved · high confidence.** Find the common acceleration including pulley inertia, then multiply sliding friction by the block displacement.

**Assumptions**

- The system starts from rest with m2 moving downward, the string is taut and does not slip, and the masses have not reached an endpoint. The coefficient k is used for sliding friction.

Let a be positive for m2 downward and m1 to the right. The horizontal block has normal force m1 g and sliding friction k m1 g.

$$
T_1-km_1g=m_1a,\qquad m_2g-T_2=m_2a,\qquad T_2-T_1=\frac12ma.
$$

Adding the equations gives the acceleration; the radius cancels because a uniform disc has I/R²=m/2.

$$
D=m_1+m_2+m/2,\qquad a=\frac{(m_2-km_1)g}{D}.
$$

Starting from rest, m1 travels at²/2. Friction opposes this displacement and therefore performs negative work.

$$
W_f=-km_1g\frac{at^2}{2}=-\frac{km_1g^2(m_2-km_1)t^2}{2(m_1+m_2+m/2)}.
$$

**Results**

Work of friction on m1 during the first t seconds

$$
W_f(t)=-\frac{km_1g^2(m_2-km_1)t^2}{2(m_1+m_2+m/2)}
$$

For release from rest followed by downward motion of m2, requiring m2>k m1 within this friction model.

**Limitations**

- If a nonzero initial descending speed v0 is intended, replace at²/2 by v0 t+at²/2 and use the result only while sliding continues in that direction.
- The statement prescribes descending motion. Release from rest with m2≤k m1 does not produce that motion; a separate static-friction coefficient would be needed to decide whether the system remains at rest in a general onset-of-motion question.

## 1.248

**Problem statement**

A uniform cylinder of radius R is spinned about its axis to the angular velocity ω_0 and then placed into a corner (Fig. 1.58). The coefficient of friction between the corner walls and the cylinder is equal to k. How many turns will the cylinder accomplish before it stops?

**Solved · high confidence.** Resolve both contact reactions for the indicated spin, then integrate the constant frictional angular deceleration.

**Assumptions**

- Fig. 1.58 indicates counterclockwise spin against a left vertical wall and a horizontal floor: the bottom surface moves right and the left surface moves down. Both contacts remain closed, the centre is initially at rest, and k is the sliding-friction coefficient. Placement transients are neglected.

Denote the upward floor normal by Nf and the rightward wall normal by Nw. Floor friction is leftward k Nf and wall friction is upward k Nw. Fixed-centre force balance gives both positive reactions.

$$
N_w-kN_f=0,\qquad N_f+kN_w-mg=0,\qquad N_f=\frac{mg}{1+k^2},\quad N_w=\frac{kmg}{1+k^2}.
$$

Both friction forces oppose the indicated rotation. Use the central moment of inertia of a uniform solid cylinder.

$$
\tau=-kR(N_f+N_w)=-\frac{kmgR(1+k)}{1+k^2},\quad I=\frac12mR^2,\quad\beta=\frac{2kg(1+k)}{R(1+k^2)}.
$$

With constant deceleration magnitude β, the angle traversed before rest is ω0²/(2β). Divide by 2π to express it in turns.

$$
t_s=\omega_0/\beta,\qquad n=\frac{\omega_0^2}{4\pi\beta}=\frac{R\omega_0^2(1+k^2)}{8\pi kg(1+k)}.
$$

**Results**

Number of turns before stopping

$$
n=\frac{R\omega_0^2(1+k^2)}{8\pi kg(1+k)}
$$

For the counterclockwise spin shown in Fig. 1.58 and k>0.

**Limitations**

- The spin direction in the figure is essential. For clockwise rotation the floor friction and wall normal both point right, so a fixed centre would require a negative wall normal. That motion loses wall contact and is outside this two-contact stopping formula.
- For k=0 and ω0>0 the cylinder never stops. If ω0=0 it makes zero turns.

## 1.249

**Problem statement**

A uniform disc of radius R is spinned to the angular velocity ω and then carefully placed on a horizontal surface. How long will the disc be rotating on the surface if the friction coefficient is equal to k? The pressure exerted by the disc on the surface can be regarded as uniform.

**Solved · high confidence.** Integrate the friction torque over the uniformly loaded circular face.

**Assumptions**

- The disc lies flat on its face with its centre at rest. It is thin, uniform, and initially spins with angular speed ω. Sliding friction has constant coefficient k and contact pressure is uniform.

With pressure p=mg/(πR²), a ring of radius r and width dr has normal load p 2πr dr and friction torque r times k times that load.

$$
d\tau=2\pi kp r^2dr,\qquad |\tau|=2\pi kp\int_0^Rr^2dr=\frac23kmgR.
$$

The friction torque is constant until rotation stops. Divide it by the disc moment of inertia mR²/2.

$$
\beta=\frac{|\tau|}{I}=\frac{4kg}{3R},\qquad t_s=\frac{\omega}{\beta}=\frac{3R\omega}{4kg}.
$$

**Results**

Time until rotation stops

$$
t_s=\frac{3R\omega}{4kg}
$$

For k>0 and initial angular speed ω≥0.

**Limitations**

- For k=0 and ω>0 there is no stopping. A nonuniform pressure distribution would require its own torque integral.

## 1.250

**Problem statement**

A flywheel with the initial angular velocity ω_0 decelerates due to the forces whose moment relative to the axis is proportional to the square root of its angular velocity. Find the mean angular velocity of the flywheel averaged over the total deceleration time.

**Solved · high confidence.** Integrate the square-root deceleration law and average the resulting angular velocity over the stopping interval.

**Assumptions**

- The moment of inertia is constant and the braking torque is −C√ω for ω>0, with C>0. The flywheel remains at rest after it stops.

Write c=C/I. The angular equation separates, and the nonnegative solution decreases to zero in finite time.

$$
\dot\omega=-c\sqrt\omega,\qquad \sqrt{\omega(t)}=\sqrt{\omega_0}-ct/2,\qquad t_s=\frac{2\sqrt{\omega_0}}c.
$$

Express the speed in terms of the stopping time and integrate directly.

$$
\omega(t)=\omega_0(1-t/t_s)^2,\qquad\overline\omega=\frac1{t_s}\int_0^{t_s}\omega_0(1-t/t_s)^2dt=\frac{\omega_0}{3}.
$$

**Results**

Mean angular velocity over the full deceleration

$$
\overline\omega=\frac13\omega_0
$$

## 1.251

**Problem statement**

A uniform cylinder of radius R and mass M can rotate freely about a stationary horizontal axis O (Fig. 1.59). A thin cord of length l and mass m is wound on the cylinder in a single layer. Find the angular acceleration of the cylinder as a function of the length x of the hanging part of the cord. The wound part of the cord is supposed to have its centre of gravity on the cylinder axis.

**Solved · high confidence.** Include both wound and hanging cord in the angular momentum or energy of the complete system.

**Assumptions**

For 0≤x≤l while the stated winding and taut-cord constraints remain valid.

**Problem statement**

- The resultant normal force is free to shift within the base; the translational solution requires k≤1.
- The axis is fixed in the plane, perpendicular to the motion; take x along the initial velocity and z upward.

Friction acts at ground level and tends to rotate the cube about its centre. Pure translation requires an opposite moment from the normal force, whose line must lie ahead of the centre.

$$
f=kmg,\qquad N\delta-f\frac a2=0,\quad N=mg\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\delta=\frac{ka}{2}.
$$

The cube has orbital angular momentum about a ground-level transverse axis despite having no spin. With y chosen so that r×p is positive y, this momentum is proportional to its speed.

$$
L_y=\frac{ma}{2}v,\qquad\dot v=-kg,\qquad\dot L_y=-\frac{k m g a}{2}.
$$

Gravity and the shifted normal force form the external couple about that axis; friction itself has zero moment about any axis lying in the same plane and transverse to motion.

$$
\tau_y=-mg\delta=-\frac{k m g a}{2}=\dot L_y.
$$

Thus the support-normal shift supplies exactly the torque that removes the initial orbital angular momentum during stopping.

$$
\int\tau_y\,dt=-\frac{ma}{2}v_0=-L_y(0).
$$

Distinguish the normal force from the complete support resultant. Adding friction to the normal force makes a tilted resultant whose line passes through the centre, as required by zero torque there.

$$
\mathbf R_{\rm support}=(-kmg,0,mg),\qquad\mathbf r_{P/C}=(ka/2,0,-a/2),\qquad\mathbf r_{P/C}\times\mathbf R_{\rm support}=0.
$$

**Results**

Horizontal separation of the weight and normal-reaction resultants

$$
\delta=ka/2
$$

The normal resultant is displaced forward in the direction of sliding.

Angular-momentum loss about the ground-level transverse axis

$$
\Delta L_y=-ma v_0/2
$$

Produced by the couple of gravity and the shifted normal reaction.

Distance if reaction means the complete normal-plus-friction resultant

$$
0
$$

Its line intersects the weight line at the cube centre; ka/2 refers to the two vertical resultant lines.

**Limitations**

- For k>1 the required normal resultant would lie outside the base, so tipping must be included and pure translational stopping is not consistent.
- The printed word reaction can mean either the normal reaction or all contact forces; both interpretations are explicitly distinguished.

At an endpoint with no tilt velocity the sphere retains its original axial spin energy, but it also has transverse rotational energy. Motor work is the total increase.

$$
K_f=\frac12 I_0\omega_0^2+\frac12(I+I_0\sin^2\theta)\Omega^2,\qquad W(\theta)=\frac{I_0^2\omega_0^2(1-\cos\theta)^2}{2(I+I_0\sin^2\theta)}.
$$

Evaluate the endpoint work for the two requested tilts.

$$
W_{90}=\frac{I_0^2\omega_0^2}{2(I+I_0)},\qquad W_{180}=\frac{2I_0^2\omega_0^2}{I}.
$$

At ninety degrees the sphere’s horizontal angular momentum is I0ω0n and rotates with the platform. The support must provide its horizontal time derivative.

$$
\mathbf N_{\rm ext}=\Omega\mathbf z\times(I_0\omega_0\mathbf n),\qquad N_{90}=\frac{I_0^2\omega_0^2}{I+I_0}.
$$

**Results**

Motor work for a 90-degree tilt

$$
W_{90}=\frac{I_0^2\omega_0^2}{2(I+I_0)}
$$

Motor work for a 180-degree tilt

$$
W_{180}=\frac{2I_0^2\omega_0^2}{I}
$$

External support torque after the 90-degree tilt

$$
\mathbf N_{\rm ext}=\frac{I_0^2\omega_0^2}{I+I_0}(\mathbf z\times\mathbf n)
$$

Horizontal, perpendicular to the shaft; the arrangement exerts the opposite torque on its support.

**Limitations**

- A nonzero final tilt speed would add ½I0 θdot² to the motor work. The given work values assume the motor stops the tilting motion at each requested angle.

## 1.281

**Problem statement**

A horizontally oriented uniform rod AB of mass m = 1.40 kg and length l_0 = 100 cm rotates freely about a stationary vertical axis OO' passing through its end A. The point A is located at the middle of the axis OO' whose length is equal to l = 55 cm. At what angular velocity of the rod the horizontal component of the force acting on the lower end of the axis OO' is equal to zero? What is in this case the horizontal component of the force acting on the upper end of the axis?

**Solved · high confidence.** Balance the radial force and transverse torque of the rotating rod and its vertical shaft.

**Assumptions**

- The axis is light and its bearing reactions act at its upper and lower ends, each l/2 above or below A. The rod centre lies at l0/2 from A.
- Define er outward from the axis along the rod and eφ=z×er. Use g=9.81 m/s².

The rod rotates in a horizontal plane; its angular momentum about A is constant and vertical. Its net torque about A is therefore zero. Gravity produces a transverse moment.

$$
\mathbf r_C=\frac{l_0}{2}\mathbf e_r,\qquad \mathbf N_g=\frac{mgl_0}{2}\mathbf e_\varphi.
$$

Let U and L be the signed outward radial bearing forces on the shaft. Their sum accelerates the rod centre and their difference cancels the gravity moment.

$$
U+L=-\frac{m\omega^2l_0}{2},\qquad \frac l2(U-L)+\frac{mgl_0}{2}=0.
$$

Set the lower radial force to zero and solve the two equations.

$$
L=0,\quad U=-\frac{mgl_0}{l},\qquad \omega=\sqrt{\frac{2g}{l}}=5.9727\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}.
$$

Insert the mass and lengths to find the upper horizontal force.

$$
|U|=\frac{(1.40)(9.81)(1.00)}{0.55}=24.9709\ \mathrm N.
$$

**Results**

Angular speed making the lower horizontal force zero

$$
\omega=\sqrt{\frac{2g}{l}}=5.97\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}
$$

Upper horizontal force on the shaft

$$
\mathbf F_U=-\frac{mgl_0}{l}\mathbf e_r,\qquad |F_U|=25.0\ \mathrm N
$$

The upper bearing pushes the shaft inward, opposite the rod’s outward radial direction; the shaft pushes the bearing outward.

## 1.282

**Problem statement**

The middle of a uniform rod of mass m and length l is rigidly fixed to a vertical axis OO' so that the angle between the rod and the axis is equal to θ (see Fig. 1.71). The ends of the axis OO' are provided with bearings. The system rotates without friction with an angular velocity ω. Find: (a) the magnitude and direction of the rod's angular momentum M relative to the point C, as well as its angular momentum relative to the rotation axis; (b) how much the modulus of the vector M relative to the point C increases during a half-turn; (c) the moment of external forces N acting on the axle OO' in the process of rotation.

**Solved · medium confidence.** Use the inertia tensor of the inclined rod, then distinguish the change of angular-momentum magnitude from the magnitude of its vector change.

**Assumptions**

- The rod is thin, with midpoint C fixed on the vertical axis. Define n=sinθ er+cosθ z toward its upper end, eφ=z×er, and ω>0 along z. The axle’s own transverse angular momentum is negligible.

The rod has zero axial inertia along n and moment J about any perpendicular axis through C.

$$
J=\frac{ml^2}{12},\qquad \mathbf M=J(\mathbf 1-\mathbf n\mathbf n)\omega\mathbf z=J\omega(\sin^2\theta\,\mathbf z-\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_r).
$$

Its angular momentum is perpendicular to the rod, in the rod-axis plane and on the side of the vertical opposite the upper rod’s horizontal projection.

$$
|\mathbf M|=J\omega\sin\theta,\qquad M_z=J\omega\sin^2\theta,\qquad \angle(\mathbf M,\mathbf z)=\frac\pi2-\theta\quad(0<\theta\leq\pi/2).
$$

A half-turn reverses er without changing either coefficient. Thus the literal modulus does not increase, whereas the vector has a finite increment.

$$
\Delta|\mathbf M|=0,\qquad \Delta\mathbf M=2J\omega\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_r(0),\qquad |\Delta\mathbf M|=J\omega|\sin2\theta|.
$$

Differentiate the rotating horizontal component to find the external support torque required for the complete arrangement.

$$
\frac{d\mathbf M}{dt}=-J\omega^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_\varphi=\mathbf N_{\rm support\to arrangement}.
$$

The axle transmits this torque to the rod. The rod exerts the opposite torque on the axle, balanced by the bearing couple when the axle’s own angular momentum is constant.

$$
\mathbf N_{\rm axle\to rod}=-J\omega^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_\varphi,\quad \mathbf N_{\rm rod\to axle}=+J\omega^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_\varphi.
$$

**Results**

Rod angular momentum about C

$$
M=\frac{ml^2\omega}{12}\sin\theta
$$

Direction as in the derivation; angular momentum is zero for a perfectly thin vertical rod, so then its direction is undefined.

Angular momentum about the vertical rotation axis

$$
M_z=\frac{ml^2\omega}{12}\sin^2\theta
$$

Increase of the modulus during a half-turn

$$
\Delta|\mathbf M|=0
$$

This follows the literal printed wording.

Magnitude of the vector increment during a half-turn

$$
|\Delta\mathbf M|=\frac{ml^2\omega}{12}|\sin2\theta|
$$

Included because the printed wording may intend the modulus of the increment.

External bearing/support couple on the axle and rod assembly

$$
\mathbf N_{\rm support}=-\frac{ml^2\omega^2}{12}\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_\varphi
$$

The torque exerted by the rod on the axle is opposite. The net torque on a negligibly light axle alone is zero after its support couple and rod torque are summed.

**Limitations**

- Part (b) cannot have a nonzero increase of |M| in the stated uniform motion.

Effective-gravity precession rate

$$

$$

Precession angular-velocity vector

$$
\boldsymbol\Omega^\prime=\frac{ml}{I\omega}(g\mathbf z+\mathbf a)
$$

Tilted 5.82 degrees from the upward vertical toward the block acceleration; reversed if the spin sense is reversed.

## 1.289

**Problem statement**

A locomotive is propelled by a turbine whose axle is parallel to the axes of wheels. The turbine's rotation direction coincides with that of wheels. The moment of inertia of the turbine rotor relative to its own axis is equal to I = 240 kg·m² . Find the additional force exerted by the gyroscopic forces on the rails when the locomotive moves along a circle of radius R = 250 m with velocity v = 50 km per hour. The gauge is equal to l = 1.5 m. **Assumptions**

- The locomotive makes a steady level turn. Consider only the turbine’s gyroscopic contribution, apart from static weight and centrifugal load transfer.
- Define x forward, y left, z up. Forward-rolling wheels and the turbine have spin angular momentum +Hy. A left turn has precession +Ωz.

Convert the turbine speed and locomotive speed, then find the yaw rate.

$$
\omega=\frac{2\pi(1500)}{60}=50\pi\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},\qquad v=\frac{50}{3.6}=13.8889\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\qquad \Omega=\frac{v}{R}=0.0555556\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}.
$$

The required torque on the turbine is backward along the locomotive, and its reaction on the vehicle is forward.

$$
\boldsymbol\tau_{\rm bearings\to turbine}=\Omega\mathbf z\times H\mathbf y=-\Omega H\mathbf x,\qquad \mathbf N_{\rm turbine\to vehicle}=+\Omega H\mathbf x.
$$

Equal and opposite additional vertical rail forces separated by the gauge l transmit this couple. Their magnitude is moment divided by gauge.

$$
N=I\omega\Omega=2094.40\ \mathrm{N\,m},\qquad \Delta F=\frac Nl=1396.26\ \mathrm N.
$$

$$
\Delta\mathbf F_{\rm outer}=-\Delta F\,\mathbf z,\qquad \Delta\mathbf F_{\rm inner}=+\Delta F\,\mathbf z.
$$

**Results**

Additional downward load on the outer rail

$$
\Delta F=\frac{I\omega v}{Rl}=1.40\times10^3\ \mathrm N
$$

The inner rail’s downward load decreases by the same amount. This outer-loading sense holds for either turn direction because the turbine spins like the wheels.

$$
\Delta(F_{\rm outer}-F_{\rm inner})=2\Delta F=2.79\times10^3\ \mathrm N
$$

The per-rail load increment, rather than this difference, is the force forming the couple.
